Teenagers are often perfectly capable speakers who go quiet the moment an activity feels like "a language exercise." The content matters less than most teachers assume — what matters more is whether the format gives them a genuine reason to speak, rather than an obvious excuse to perform for the teacher.

A few things consistently help.

## Give them a role, not just a topic

Simply asking "What do you think about social media?" invites a shrug. Assigning a clear position — as in **Debate Cards**, where teams argue "for" or "against" a statement regardless of their real opinion — removes the personal pressure of sharing an opinion and replaces it with a task to complete. Paradoxically, that often produces more honest, energetic discussion than asking directly.

## Let the body move before the mouth does

**Opinion Line**, where students physically stand along an imaginary line to show how strongly they agree or disagree with a statement, gets students committing to a position before they have to explain it. By the time they're asked to speak, they've already made a choice — the hard part is done, and the talking follows naturally.

## Use unpredictability to your advantage

Teenagers often disengage from anything that feels scripted. **Discussion Dice**, where a roll decides which question gets answered next, keeps the activity slightly unpredictable and prevents anyone from simply reciting a memorised answer.

## Start with low-stakes personal sharing

Early in a course, before deeper discussion topics land well, something like **Two Truths and a Lie** builds the habit of speaking about themselves in a format that's inherently playful rather than earnest — which tends to lower the guard of even reluctant speakers.

## The common thread

None of these activities ask teenagers to simply "discuss" in the abstract. Each one gives a structure, a role, or a small game mechanic that makes speaking the natural next step, not an assignment.
