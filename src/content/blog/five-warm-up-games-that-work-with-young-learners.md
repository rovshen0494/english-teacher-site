With young learners, the first five minutes of a lesson matter more than almost any other part of it. Start with something too quiet or too complicated, and you spend the rest of the hour trying to win back attention. Start with something active, clear and genuinely fun, and the whole class tends to fall into place.

Here's what makes a warm-up actually work at this age, and a few reliable options.

## Keep instructions short and physical

Young learners understand actions faster than explanations. A warm-up that can be demonstrated in ten seconds — rather than explained in a paragraph — will always work better than one that needs careful setup. **Simon Says** is a good example: one quick demonstration, and the whole class understands the rules immediately.

## Use movement to burn off early energy

Especially in the first lesson of the day or after a break, young learners often arrive with energy that needs somewhere to go before they can focus. A quick round of **Colour Hunt** — running to touch a called-out colour — channels that energy productively while quietly reviewing vocabulary at the same time.

## Build in repetition without it feeling repetitive

Vocabulary at this age needs a lot of repeated exposure, but repeating the same drill directly gets boring fast. **Animal Actions** solves this by pairing each word with a distinct physical action, so saying "monkey" for the fifth time still feels different because the movement, not just the word, is doing some of the work.

## Give quieter students an easy way in

Not every child wants to be the centre of attention in the first five minutes. A short, low-pressure memory game like **What's Missing?** lets every student participate just by looking and thinking, with speaking staying optional until they're ready.

## Keep it under ten minutes

A warm-up that runs too long stops being a warm-up. Keep these activities short and lead directly into the main lesson content while energy and attention are both still high.
