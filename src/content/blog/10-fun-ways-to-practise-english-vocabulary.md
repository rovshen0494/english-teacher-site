Vocabulary lists are easy to hand out and easy to forget. A word written down once, translated once, and never used again rarely survives past the following week. What actually builds lasting vocabulary is repetition in different, meaningful contexts — hearing it, saying it, searching for it, and using it in a real sentence.

Here are a few principles that make vocabulary practice genuinely effective, along with some classroom activities built around them.

## Make it physical

Movement helps memory. Activities where students search, sort or race to find vocabulary tend to produce stronger recall than sitting and copying a word list. A **Vocabulary Treasure Hunt**, where students find hidden cards around the room and use each word in a sentence, combines physical activity with immediate spoken practice.

## Make it social

Vocabulary used in conversation with a classmate is far more memorable than vocabulary read silently. Pair and small-group games — like a quick round of **Word Bingo** or a **Vocabulary Relay** — turn recall into something social and slightly competitive, which keeps attention high without adding pressure.

## Make it repeatable, not repetitive

The same ten words reviewed three times in three different games will stick better than thirty new words seen once. A short **Memory Challenge** with a fixed set of picture-word pairs is a good five-minute warm-up to return to across a week, reinforcing the same vocabulary in a slightly different format each time.

## Let students produce, not just recognise

Recognising a word on a flashcard is a much lower bar than producing it unprompted in a sentence. Wherever possible, build in a moment where students have to generate the word themselves — describing it, using it in a sentence, or explaining it to a partner — rather than simply pointing to the right picture.

## A few more ideas to try

- Group new words into categories together as a class, and ask students to justify their groupings.
- Revisit last week's vocabulary briefly at the start of a new lesson, before introducing anything new.
- Encourage students to notice and collect vocabulary from outside class — a menu, a sign, a game — and share it the following lesson.

None of these ideas require expensive materials or long preparation. What they have in common is that students are doing something with the language, not just looking at it.
