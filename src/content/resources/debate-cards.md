## Learning Objective

Students build and deliver a structured argument, using persuasive language to support an assigned position.

## Materials

A set of debate statements appropriate to teenage interests (e.g. "Social media does more harm than good"), and cards assigning "For" or "Against."

## Preparation

Choose 4-6 statements likely to generate genuine opinions, and prepare position cards for two teams per statement.

## How It Works

1. Present a debate statement and split the class into two teams, assigning "For" and "Against" positions (not necessarily their real opinion).
2. Give teams 5-8 minutes to prepare two or three supporting points together.
3. Each team presents their opening argument, alternating speakers.
4. Allow a short rebuttal round where each team responds to one point from the other side.
5. Hold a brief class vote on which argument was most convincing, separate from personal opinion on the topic.

## Student Instructions

Work with your team to build your argument, even if it's not what you personally believe. Use phrases like "I believe that..." and "This is because..."

## Variations

**Easier:** Provide a list of useful argument phrases and a few starter points on the board.

**Harder:** Require each team to use at least one piece of evidence or example, not just opinion.

## Extension Activity

Students write a short paragraph individually, arguing the opposite position to the one they debated.

## Teacher Tip

Assigning positions rather than letting students choose often produces stronger, more objective arguments — it forces genuine language work rather than simply repeating a pre-held opinion.
